How to read the certified register before signing a jeonse lease: Check the owner, mortgages, and guarantee insurance eligibility

Seongwoo Choi · 2026.11.09 · 5 min read

This article explains how to read the certified register before a jeonse lease, check ownership and senior claims, compare deposits with the property value, and assess guarantee eligibility without declaring a specific home safe.

  • 2. Section A: Check whether the lessor is the actual owner
  • 3. Section B: Check debts and other rights

The first document to check in a jeonse lease is the certified register (full certificate of registered matters). It shows on one page who owns the property, how much debt is recorded against it, and whether other parties have rights over it. This article explains the order for reading the certified register before signing and how to calculate senior claims relative to the deposit. It does not determine whether a specific home is safe; the final judgment should be confirmed before signing through a licensed real estate agent, institutional screening, and consultation with a professional.

1. Title section: Check that it is the home you are contracting for

Compare the address and unit number, building use, and exclusive area in the register with the lease contract. If the address is written with a different unit number, stop the contract and verify it. Guidance from HUG explains that buildings not intended for residential use, such as neighborhood living facilities, may make it difficult to obtain a jeonse loan or guarantee. For condominium-type buildings such as apartments, the building and land are shown on one page, but for multi-household, multi-family, and detached houses, you may need to check the land register separately.

2. Section A: Check whether the lessor is the actual owner

The owner listed lowest in Section A is the current owner. Compare the lessor's name and address in the contract with the registered information. In the same section, look for seizures, provisional seizures, provisional dispositions, decisions commencing compulsory auction proceedings, and trust records. HUG guidance explains that when a trust is registered, the lessor cannot enter into a lease without the trust company's consent. If any of these records appear, verify the facts with a professional before signing.

3. Section B: Check debts and other rights

Section B records collateral security rights, jeonse rights, and registered leasehold rights. The maximum secured claim amount of a collateral security right is the maximum amount the secured creditor may receive, and it cannot be assumed to equal the actual outstanding loan balance. If you obtain a copy including canceled entries, you can also see rights canceled in the past and check for signs of disputes over previous tenants' deposits. Anyone, even a person who is not the lessor, can view the register through the Supreme Court Internet Registry Office.

4. Calculation example: Compare senior claims and the deposit with the property value

Let us calculate using hypothetical figures. Assume a property value of KRW 300 million, a maximum secured claim of KRW 100 million for a senior collateral security right, my jeonse deposit of KRW 150 million, and no senior tenant. The senior claim and deposit total KRW 250 million, or approximately 83% of the property value (2.5 ÷ 3). The Korea Housing Finance Corporation's guidance on its General Jeonse Protection Guarantee uses this ratio as the loan-to-value ratio (LTV) and explains that a guarantee fee rate of 0.18% per year applies to the range above 80% and up to 90%.

Using the same assumptions, the guarantee fee is approximately KRW 540,000, calculated as KRW 150 million × 0.18% per year × 2 years (730 days ÷ 365). The actual amount may differ if preferential discounts or rate changes apply. These figures are an example intended to show the calculation method and are not the guarantee fee for an actual property.

The same guidance explains that the guarantee limit is the lower of the regional ceiling (KRW 700 million in the Seoul metropolitan area and KRW 500 million in other areas) and 90% of the housing price minus senior claims. For multi-household and detached houses, separate limits by housing type apply to the total senior claims and the amount secured by collateral, so you should not conclude that you are eligible based on this example alone.

5. For multi-household homes, check other households' deposits

In a multi-household home, even if there is one lessor, the deposits of multiple households in the building may compete for priority with the deposit of the same owner. The amount of other households' deposits cannot be determined from the certified register alone. HUG guidance explains that the lessor's consent is required to view the status of fixed-date registrations, and, if consent cannot be obtained, introduces a method of viewing the fixed dates of households registered as moving into a multi-household home at the community service center after signing the contract. If the results indicate risk, it is advisable to agree in advance with the agent on the ability to request cancellation of the contract under a special clause.

6. Pre-contract checklist

① Check whether the address, unit number, and area in the contract match the title section of the register. ② Check whether the current owner in Section A is the same as the lessor, and confirm that there are no records of seizure, provisional seizure, provisional disposition, auction, or trust. ③ Add the maximum secured claim amount of collateral security rights in Section B, senior tenant deposits, and my deposit, and compare the total with the property value. ④ For a multi-household home, ask whether the lessor's consent is required to check the status of fixed-date registrations. ⑤ Unpaid national taxes and unpaid wages may not appear in the register, so request proof of full payment or similar documents from the lessor. ⑥ Determine whether the contract term is at least one year and when to file the move-in report and obtain the fixed date.

HF guidance states that a guarantee application must be made before half of the contract term has passed. The application timing and required documents vary by product and provider, so check the provider's guidance again before applying.
